We often picture courage as physical daring — facing danger, taking risks, standing in harm's way. Twain observes that this kind of bravery is surprisingly common; people face physical danger every day. Moral courage is rarer: standing by an unpopular truth, defending someone others have condemned, or admitting a fault when silence would be easier. It costs reputation, comfort, and sometimes friendship, which is why so few practise it. The quote asks us to notice which kind of courage we admire and which kind we actually need — and to cultivate the rarer one.
